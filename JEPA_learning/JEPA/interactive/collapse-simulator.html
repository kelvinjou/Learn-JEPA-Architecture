Diagnostics

std ratio is the mean per-channel std of ℓ₂-normalised embeddings, divided by 1/√D. Healthy ≈ 0.7–1.0; under 0.05 is collapsed. rank counts embedding directions above 1% of the top singular value, out of D = 16. probe is a ridge linear probe on 4 classes. ‖z‖ is the mean embedding norm — watch it when you turn the target LayerNorm off. Chance is 25%, but the number that matters is the untrained encoder, averaged over three random initialisations: –. Note how high that is. A random 2-layer MLP is a surprisingly good feature extractor, and any claim that does not beat it is not a claim.

The setup

16×16 images, 4×4 patch grid, four shape classes. Encoder is a 2-layer MLP per patch (16 → 32 → 16); the context representation is the mean of visible patch embeddings; the predictor is a 2-layer MLP on [context ; block position]. No attention, so every gradient is hand-derivable and you can read the code. Everything that matters for collapse is present: asymmetry, stop-gradient, EMA, a latent-space loss, a predictor.

What to watch Panel (a) and panel (b) tell opposite stories about the red curve. In a joint-embedding architecture the loss is not merely uninformative — it is anti-correlated with representation quality, because the cheapest way to make two embeddings agree is to make every embedding identical.
Three experiments 1. Set EMA m to 0 and watch the green curve become the blue one. It does not collapse — stop-gradient alone is enough, which is SimSiam's finding: the EMA is "not directly related to preventing collapsing". Whether EMA also helps takes a few thousand steps to settle here; below about 2000 steps the two are within noise of each other, and after 4000 the EMA run is usually ahead. Do not read a 2-point gap at step 300 as a result.
2. Set m to 1. The target is now a frozen untrained network. No collapse, but a low ceiling: you are predicting random features.
3. Turn the target LayerNorm off and watch the ‖z‖ column. Collapse changes route: with the LayerNorm on, the no-stop-gradient run makes every embedding point the same direction (std ratio → 0.01, ‖z‖ stays large); with it off, it instead shrinks the embedding scale towards zero (‖z‖ → 0.05) while the std ratio stays deceptively respectable. Two different degeneracies, one loss function, and the std-ratio diagnostic only catches one of them.

Linear-model dynamics

Tian, Chen & Ganguli reduce the whole question to two scalars per eigenmode: p, the predictor's eigenvalue, and s, the eigenvalue of F = WXWᵀ (the online network's output covariance). With a stop-gradient in place,

ṗ = αp s [τ − (1+σ²)p] − ηp
ṡ = 2ps [τ − (1+σ²)p] − 2ηs

The prediction to check At η = 0.05, σ² = 0.5, τ = 1 the theory says the basin edge sits at p*₋ = 0.05445. Click just left and just right of the dotted red line, on the parabola, and see that it is right. My numerical integration agrees at four significant figures — see Figure 5.1 of the book and exp/tian_dynamics.py.
Now push η past ηcrit Above τ²/[4(1+σ²)] the two positive fixed points annihilate in a saddle-node bifurcation and zero is the only attractor left. Every initialisation collapses, no matter how well you tune anything else. That is why the weight-decay schedule in I-JEPA matters, and why it ramps up rather than starting high.
